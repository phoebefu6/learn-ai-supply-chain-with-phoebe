Constructed Northwick example. One best-selling lantern, one week. 1,200 units arrive at the DCs; the channels want 1,600 between them:

ChannelUnits wantedMargin a unit
North stores (DC North)700GBP 7.50
South stores (DC South)500GBP 6.80
Online400GBP 9.00

400 units of demand will not be met this week whatever you do. The only question is where the gap lands, and what that choice costs in margin and in customers. Your numbers will differ; the arithmetic will not.

Real world

Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang's 1997 Management Science paper names a "rationing game" as one of four sources of the bullwhip effect, alongside demand signal processing, order batching and price variations. Allocation is not a back-office detail: the rule you pick changes how customers order, which changes the demand signal everyone upstream plans from.

Part 1 · covers fair share, priority and margin allocation

Four rules, one pile, four different losers

6 min live

Every allocation rule answers the same question in a different order: who gets served first? Fair share gives everyone the same fraction. Priority serves a named channel in full before the next. Margin first serves whoever earns most a unit. Floor, then margin guarantees everyone a minimum and then chases margin with what is left.

LiveFour rules, written so a planner can apply them4 min▶
RuleHow to apply it by handAt 1,200 units (constructed)
Fair shareEveryone gets the same fraction: supply divided by total demand, times what each channel wanted1,200 / 1,600 = 75% each: 525 / 375 / 300, GBP 9,188
Stores first (priority)Serve the priority list in order, each in full, until the units run out700 / 500 / 0: online gets nothing, GBP 8,650
Highest margin firstSort channels by margin a unit, serve in that orderOnline 400, North 700, South what is left: 100 (20%), GBP 9,530
Floor, then marginGive every channel a fixed share of its demand first (here 50%), then serve the rest by margin350 / 250 / 200 first, then online +200, North +200: 550 / 250 / 400, GBP 9,425

Read the last two rows together. The floor costs GBP 105 of margin against margin first (9,530 minus 9,425) and buys this: no channel below half. Whether GBP 105 is cheap or expensive is a business judgement, and it is exactly the judgement the rule should write down.

The one-line test. For any rule, ask: at this week's supply, which channel has the lowest fill, and would its manager sign the rule if they read that number first?

Self-studyEvery rule is a decision about which customers wait3 min▶

There is no neutral rule. Fair share looks neutral and still spreads the shortage onto every customer, including the channel that earns most a unit. Stores first looks like loyalty to the shops and is, in practice, a decision that online customers see "out of stock" all week. Margin first looks like good business and lands the whole gap on South, the channel with the thinnest margin, which may also be the one with the most loyal customers.

None of these is wrong. Each is a value judgement dressed as arithmetic. The professional norm is to write the judgement down, name who owns it, and review it after every shortage, so that the next short week is decided by a rule people agreed to and not by whoever was on shift.

  • Floor protects the relationship: no channel goes dark.
  • Margin protects this week's profit.
  • Tie-break settles the case the other two leave open, such as two channels with equal margin.
Part 2 · covers the rationing game

Ration on orders, and customers order more than they need

5 min live

Fair share has a catch. If customers learn that, in a shortage, everyone gets the same fraction of what they ordered, the rational move is to order more than they need. When supply comes back, the extra orders are cancelled or never repeated. They were never demand. But the order history recorded them, and next season's forecast learns from that history.

Shortage. Supply is 75% of what the channels ordered last time. Customers notice.

Ration. The rule gives every customer 75% of what it ordered. Fair, on paper.

Inflate. A store that needs 100 orders 140 so that 75% covers it. Everyone does the same.

Recover. Supply returns. The extra orders are cancelled or never repeated. They were never demand.

Forecast. The order history still shows a spike. Next season's forecast plans for it. Constructed illustration.

LiveWhy the shortage outlives itself3 min▶

Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang (Management Science, 1997) name the rationing game as one of four sources of the bullwhip effect: demand signal processing, the rationing game, order batching and price variations. The mechanism above is why it belongs on that list: the allocation rule changes how customers order, and the inflated orders travel upstream as if they were real.

Two consequences for Northwick. First, a shortage week's orders are a bad training signal: if they reach the forecast unflagged, the forecast learns the panic. Second, the bigger the shortage and the more predictable the rationing rule, the bigger the inflation. Session 4 runs the bullwhip itself on 104 real weeks; tonight you only need to know that the allocation rule is one of the levers.

Real world

The same paper reports that Procter & Gamble "found that the diaper orders issued by the distributors have a degree of variability". That is the bullwhip in one line: orders upstream swinging more than the end demand behind them, which is what the rationing game helps produce.

Self-studyAllocate on sell-through, not on orders3 min▶

Among the counteracting actions, the Management Science paper lists "information sharing of sell-through and inventory status data". For allocation that becomes a simple rule: split scarce stock by what each channel actually sold in recent weeks, not by what it ordered this week. A store that orders 140 but sold 100 a week gets its share of 100. Inflating the order earns nothing, so nobody bothers.

  • What it needs: point-of-sale and online sales by channel, and stock on hand, visible to the planner on the day of the shortage.
  • What it fixes: the incentive to inflate, and the phantom spike in next season's history.
  • What to flag anyway: mark shortage weeks in the demand history, so the forecast in session 1's playground never learns from rationed sales as if they were full demand.

The paper's other counteracting actions belong to session 4, where the information sharing is measured on a three-tier chain.

Part 3 · covers what an optimiser needs to be told

An optimiser does the arithmetic, not the judgement

6 min live

An allocation optimiser does what you just did by hand, over thousands of SKUs and locations, every day, with trucks and shelf space in the sum. That is real value. What it cannot do is choose the objective, the constraints or the tie-breaks. Those arrive as inputs, and if nobody writes them, a default in the software writes them for you.

LiveWrite the rule before you buy the solver3 min▶

A vendor demo of an allocation optimiser usually shows the output: tidy units per store, higher margin than last year's manual split. Ask what it was told:

  • Objective. Margin, fill, revenue, or a weighted mix? Margin first at 1,200 units leaves South at 20 percent in the constructed Northwick example. If the objective is pure margin, the optimiser will do the same thing, faster, across every SKU.
  • Constraints. Is there a floor per channel? Truck capacity per DC? A minimum display quantity per store? A constraint left out is a customer the solver is free to starve.
  • Tie-breaks. When two channels score the same, who goes first? If nobody says, the answer is whatever order the data happened to arrive in.

If you can write those three lines for one SKU on one short week, you can check the optimiser's output against them. If you cannot, the optimiser is not the problem yet.

Real world

The rationing game was named in 1997 for a reason that has not gone away: Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang list it beside order batching and price variations as a source of distortion that sits in how the chain is run, not in how demand behaves. A faster solver applying the same order-based rule distorts faster.

Self-studyWhen an optimiser earns its place3 min▶

The routing call mirrors session 1's spreadsheet-or-model question, and the general version lives in When AI: when classic ML wins ↗.

  • A written rule is enough when shortages are occasional, involve a handful of SKUs and channels, and happen about weekly. A planner applies floor-then-margin by hand in minutes, as you will in the build-along.
  • An optimiser earns its place when the same decision runs over many SKUs times many locations, daily, with shared constraints between them (one truck, one DC, one supplier allocation) that a person cannot hold in their head.
  • Either way, the objective, the constraints and the tie-breaks are the same three lines. The optimiser scales the rule; it does not replace writing it.

The maths of multi-location inventory optimisation is out of scope here; the concept is what matters for the buying decision.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Find your last short week. Pick one SKU that ran short in the past year. Write down how the stock was actually split between channels, and who decided. If the answer is "whoever was on shift", that is the finding.
  2. Run the four rules by hand. With that SKU's real supply, demand per channel and margin per unit, compute fair share, your own priority rule, margin first and floor then margin. Note the margin and the lowest fill for each.
  3. Look for phantom demand. In the order history around that short week, compare orders with sell-through. Did any channel order more than it sold, then cancel or skip a later order?
  4. Draft section 3 and score it. Write your own allocation rule with a floor, a tie-break and an owner, then score your sections 1 to 3 on the halfway scorecard. Fix the thinnest row first.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In the constructed Northwick example, with 1,200 units available, which rule gives online nothing?

Stores first fills North (700) and South (500) and the 1,200 units are gone: online gets 0, and margin is the lowest of the four at GBP 8,650. Margin first does the opposite and fills online first, because it earns GBP 9.00 a unit.

2 · Customers learn that in a shortage everyone gets the same fraction of what they ordered. What does that do to next season's forecast?

The extra orders vanish when supply returns, but the order history already counted them. A forecast trained on that history plans for demand that never existed. Allocating on sell-through, and flagging shortage weeks, removes both the incentive and the spike.

3 · What can an allocation optimiser not decide for you?

The optimiser does the arithmetic over thousands of SKUs and locations; that is its value. The objective, the constraints such as a floor, and the tie-breaks arrive as inputs. If nobody writes them, a software default does.
