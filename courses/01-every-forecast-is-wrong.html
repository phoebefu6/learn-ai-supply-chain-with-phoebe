Part 1 · covers bias, spread and why error is an input

Two errors with two different bills

6 min live

A forecast can be wrong in two ways. It can sit on one side of what sells, week after week: that is bias, and it is a habit you can fix. Or it can be right on average and wrong every single week: that is spread, and you cannot fix it, only pay for it, in safety stock.

LiveTwo numbers that describe any forecast4 min▶
NumberWhat it countsWhat a planner does with it
WAPEUnits the forecast got wrong, in either direction, per unit actually sold, added up over SKUs and weeksSizes the spread: the bigger it is, the more safety stock the same service costs
BiasUnits forecast minus units sold, per unit sold, with the sign keptSays which way the forecast leans: plus means stock piles up, minus means shelves empty

Two numbers, always together. A forecast with a small WAPE and a big bias is precise about the wrong level. A forecast with zero bias and a big WAPE is honest and expensive. The definitions, their traps (WAPE on intermittent demand, MAPE dividing by zero) and how to backtest them belong to Timeseries Forecasting: measuring a forecast ↗ and backtesting and metrics ↗. Here they are used, not retaught.

The one-line test. Ask of any accuracy claim: was the sign thrown away? If the slide shows only one accuracy number, you do not yet know whether the error will cost you stock or cost you sales.

Self-studyWhy "wrong" is a planning input, not a failure3 min▶

A planner who treats the forecast as a promise orders exactly the forecast and is surprised every week. A planner who treats it as a distribution orders the forecast plus a buffer sized by the spread, and corrects the level when the bias shows. The second planner is not more pessimistic. They are using the error as information.

Real world

In the playground below, "same week last year" forecasts the 45 real SKUs 38.3 percent too high over the 26 holdout weeks. These products sold less in the second half of 2011 than in the same weeks of 2010. Nothing about that forecast was careless; it simply assumed last year would repeat. Bias is often a stale assumption, not a bad person.

Part 2 · covers ABC/XYZ segmentation

Not every SKU deserves the same forecast

5 min live

Four thousand SKUs do not get four thousand forecasting decisions. They get nine. Cut once by how much money each SKU makes (ABC) and once by how jumpy its weekly demand is (XYZ), and each of the nine cells earns its own method, its own service target and its own tolerance for error.

LiveHow to cut the catalogue in two passes3 min▶
  • ABC by value. Sort SKUs by revenue over the fit period, largest first. The ones that make the first 80 percent are A, the next 15 percent B, the rest C. Value, not units: a lantern that sells 50 a week at a high price outranks a card that sells 500 at a low one.
  • XYZ by volatility. For each SKU, divide the standard deviation of weekly demand by its mean: the coefficient of variation, CV. Low CV is steady (X), high is jumpy (Z).
  • Cross them. Nine cells. AX items are worth a lot and behave; CZ items are worth little and behave badly. They deserve different methods, service levels and attention, which is the point.

Set the cut from your own catalogue. Textbook XYZ thresholds (often CV 0.5 and 1.0) were written for steady consumer demand. On this wholesale-heavy retailer they put 2 SKUs in X and 1,729 in Z, which is no segmentation at all. Thirds of your own CV distribution always give three usable groups.

Self-studyWhy this real catalogue is so jumpy3 min▶

The data behind every number on this page is the UCI "Online Retail II" dataset: 1,067,371 invoice lines from a UK-based online retailer of giftware and homeware, December 2009 to December 2011, released under CC BY 4.0. The dataset card notes that "many customers of the company are wholesalers". A wholesaler buys 24 lanterns once a month, so one SKU's weekly demand looks like a comb: zero, zero, 24, zero. That is real and it is why CVs above 2 are normal here.

Northwick, the constructed retailer in every build-along, sells through stores and online, so its weekly demand would be smoother than this. The method does not change; the thresholds do. Your numbers will differ.

Real world

Invoices whose code starts with "C" are cancellations in this dataset. The weekly series here net them in, so a 1,000-unit order cancelled the same week counts as zero, not as a spike. Every real demand series has a cleaning rule like this one, and every rule moves the error you measure. Write yours down.

Part 3 · covers where AI forecasting earns its place

Where a learned model earns its place, and where a spreadsheet wins

6 min live

A learned model is worth paying for when there are many SKUs to learn from, when demand moves with things a spreadsheet cannot see (promotions, weather, a competitor's price), and when the history is long enough to learn a shape. On a few steady SKUs with short history, a moving average is hard to beat and costs nothing.

LiveThe strongest public evidence, and why it is not your number4 min▶

The M5 competition asked teams to forecast 42,840 Walmart daily unit-sales series (30,490 of them product by store) from 2011 to 2016. The competition paper (Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos, International Journal of Forecasting, 2022) reports that the top methods "focused on LightGBM implementations and variants", that the top five beat the best benchmark by "higher than 20%", and that the winner beat it by 22.4 percent. The benchmark was exponential smoothing, the same family as one of the methods in tonight's playground.

Read the same paper for the other half: of 5,507 teams, 415 (7.5 percent) beat that benchmark. Most entrants, many of them using machine learning, did not. And M5 had five years of daily history, prices, events and a product and store hierarchy to learn from. Tonight's 45 SKUs with 78 weeks of fit history are a much harder place to learn. Expect your own number to sit between the two, and measure it.

These figures are from the 2020 preprint of the paper; check them against the published version before you quote them in a contract.

Self-studySpreadsheet, classic model or AI: the general routing call3 min▶

Whether a problem needs a dashboard, a classic model or something larger is a general skill with its own course: When AI: when classic ML wins ↗. In supply chain planning the call usually lands like this:

  • A spreadsheet method wins when SKUs are few and steady, history is short, and nothing outside the series drives demand. The playground's AX and BX cells.
  • A learned model earns its place when there are many related SKUs to learn shared patterns from, demand is jumpy, and outside signals (promotions, events, price) are recorded. How those models work is taught in Timeseries Forecasting: ML forecasting ↗.
  • Promotions are the single biggest outside signal in retail. Estimating how much a price cut moves demand is its own discipline: Economics: demand from data ↗. Here they appear only as a spike the forecast should have expected.
Build-along

Northwick's forecast-error budget, from 45 real SKUs

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The playground runs five forecasters one week ahead on 45 real products, 5 from each ABC x XYZ cell, and scores them on the last 26 weeks of the data, which none of them saw while fitting. Everything is computed in your browser. You read three things from it and write the first section of Northwick's playbook.

Set the bar with a spreadsheet. Pick "8-week average". WAPE reads 79.6%, bias -4.4%. That is the best of the four spreadsheet methods overall, and the number any AI tool must beat. Then pick "Naive (last week)": 90.9%. A vendor who compares against naive is choosing the easier opponent.

★ The bar best spreadsheet method overall = 8-week average WAPE 79.6%, bias -4.4% (naive: 90.9%)

Run the learned model. Pick "Learned model". WAPE drops to 77.5%, which the meter reads as +2.7% against the 8-week average. Now read the bias: -17.3%. It is more accurate and it runs short. Session 2 shows what a forecast that runs 17 percent short does to service.

★ The learned model WAPE 77.5% (+2.7% vs the bar, +14.8% vs naive) bias -17.3%: forecasts sit below what sold

Read the grid, cell by cell. Olive cells are where the learned model beats the best spreadsheet method in that cell; yellow where it does not. It wins 6 of 9: its best is BZ (+10.0%), and it loses AX to exponential smoothing and CY to same-week-last-year (-8.9%). Open "Look at one SKU" and pick an AX item, then a BZ item, to see why.

★ Where it earns its place wins: AY +2.8 AZ +3.6 BY +4.5 BZ +10.0 CX +0.2 CZ +2.3 loses: AX -2.1 (smoothing) BX -1.4 (smoothing) CY -8.9 (last year)

Try the planner's habit. With the learned model on, drag the override to +15%. Bias improves to -4.9%, and WAPE gets worse: 79.8%, now behind the 8-week average. A blanket correction fixes the lean and adds error everywhere it was not needed. Session 5 is about doing this properly. Set it back to 0.

Write the budget into the playbook. Copy this into section 1 of your own version of Northwick's playbook and replace the numbers with yours when you have them.

Homework

Before session 2

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A vendor reports its forecast is 14.8 percent more accurate. On the same 26 weeks it is 2.7 percent better than your 8-week average. What explains the gap?

Both numbers are true on the playground. Naive scores 90.9 percent WAPE, the 8-week average 79.6, the learned model 77.5. The improvement depends entirely on the opponent, which is why the playbook names the bar.

2 · The learned model has the lowest WAPE and a bias of -17.3 percent. What does the bias tell a planner that WAPE does not?

WAPE throws the sign away. Bias keeps it: minus means the forecast leans low, so a stock policy built on it empties shelves more often than its service target promises. Session 2 measures exactly that.

3 · On the playground, where does a free spreadsheet method do as well as or better than the learned model?

AX and BX go to exponential smoothing, CY to same-week-last-year, and CX is a tie. The learned model earns its place on the jumpy cells, best on BZ at +10.0 percent.
