Part 0

Where the swing comes from

Sessions 1 to 3 planned one tier: Northwick's own stores and online shop. Real chains have more. Northwick's stores order from its two distribution centres, the centres order from suppliers, and the suppliers order from factories. Each tier sees only the orders of the tier below, and that is enough to turn a wobble into a whip.

LiveWhy this belongs in an AI planning playbook3 min▶

Every AI planning tool forecasts something. The bullwhip decides what it is forecasting. A DC model trained on the orders stores send it is learning the stores' ordering habits, not customer demand. A better model of the wrong signal is still a model of the wrong signal.

Real world

Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang open their 1997 Management Science paper with a consumer goods case: Procter & Gamble "found that the diaper orders issued by the distributors have a degree of variability" far above what babies, a famously steady source of demand, could explain. Retail sales were steady; the orders upstream swung.

Part 1 · covers the bullwhip effect and its four causes

Every tier is rational and the chain is not

6 min live

The bullwhip effect is the growth in order variability as you move up a supply chain, away from the customer. Nobody has to make a mistake for it to happen. Each tier forecasts from the orders it receives, adds a safety buffer, and orders the difference. Each step is textbook practice. Stacked, they amplify.

LiveFour causes, each a sensible local rule4 min▶

Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang's Management Science paper names "four sources of the bullwhip effect: demand signal processing, rationing game, order batching, and price variations". In planner's language:

CauseThe local ruleWhere it shows on the lever
Demand signal processingForecast from the orders you receive and reset your target level each week"React to 4 weeks": a shorter view chases every wobble
Rationing gameIn a shortage, order more than you need because you expect to be rationedSession 3's phantom demand
Order batchingOrder once a month to fill a truck or save admin"Stores order monthly"
Price variationsBuy ahead when the price is low, stop when it is highNot on the lever; see below

The one-line test. Ask of any order stream an AI tool will learn from: is it customer demand, or someone's reaction to it? If it is a reaction, the model inherits the reaction.

Self-studyPromotions: the cause a forecaster can see coming3 min▶

A supplier's price promotion makes the retailer buy weeks of stock at once, then nothing. Customer demand has not moved; orders have. Inside a retailer, a promotion also moves real customer demand, and how much a price cut moves it is an estimation problem with its own discipline: Economics: demand from data ↗. For the playbook, the rule is simpler: a promotion is a known event, so it belongs in the forecast as an input, not in the error as a surprise.

Part 2 · covers information sharing as the cure

Share what customers bought, not what the tier below ordered

6 min live

If every tier forecasts from the same customer signal, the amplification has much less to feed on. The paper's counteracting actions start with "information sharing of sell-through and inventory status data". The bench shows how much that is worth on real demand, and what it does not fix.

LiveWhat sharing fixes, and what it cannot4 min▶
  • It fixes the signal. Upstream tiers stop forecasting the downstream tier's reactions. On the lever, the DC drops from 18.2x to 9.2x and the supplier from 87.6x to 16.5x.
  • It does not remove the policy. Every tier still resets a target level each week and still orders the difference, so some amplification stays. 16.5x is still a lot.
  • It does not shorten lead times. A longer lead time means a bigger target level to reset, and every change in the forecast moves it further. That lever is in Part 3.

Sharing is cheap to describe and hard to do: it needs a data feed both companies trust, agreed definitions, and a reason for the supplier to believe the retailer's numbers. That is a contract and a relationship, not a model.

Self-studyWhat the two 1997 papers say, and what we could read3 min▶

The same authors published twice in 1997: the Management Science paper with the formal model, read in full for every quote on this page, and a shorter Sloan Management Review article, "The Bullwhip Effect in Supply Chains", written for managers. The SMR text could not be read at source here. Secondary summaries report that it groups the remedies into themes, but those labels are not quoted on this page until someone reads the original.

The managerial point survives either way: the causes are structural, so the remedies are structural too. Better forecasting at one tier helps that tier; it does not stop the tier above from forecasting your orders.

Part 3 · covers disruption sensing and control-tower claims

"Control tower" claims: test what they shorten

5 min live

Every major planning vendor now sells disruption sensing: an AI that reads news, weather, port data and supplier signals, and a "control tower" that shows it all on one screen. There is no independent public evaluation of these claims that could be read for this session. Treat them as contested, and ask the one question you can test: which delay does it shorten?

LiveFour questions for a control-tower demo3 min▶
  1. Which delay, by how much? Detection, decision or lead time, in days, measured on a past disruption of ours.
  2. What does it share? If it shows our suppliers our sell-through, it attacks the bullwhip. If it shows us their news feed, it attacks detection only.
  3. Who acts on the alert? A signal nobody owns is a dashboard. Name the role and the decision it triggers.
  4. What did it miss? Ask for the false alarms and the misses from a past period, not the highlight reel.
Self-studyWhy the evidence is thin, and how to hold it3 min▶

Disruption sensing is hard to evaluate in public: disruptions are rare, each is different, and a vendor's case study picks the event it caught. The honest position is that the category is contested. Some teams report faster responses; nobody has published the misses alongside the catches in a form that could be read here. Run your own back-test on two or three past disruptions before you sign, and write the result down whichever way it goes.

Real world

The lever's "Lead time 4 weeks" rung is the cheapest experiment on this page. Doubling the lead time from 2 to 4 weeks lifts the DC from 18.2x to 40.1x and the supplier to 289.6x. Shortening a supplier's lead time is a negotiation, not an algorithm, and on this chain it is worth more than any forecast improvement.

Build-along

Break the chain, then repair it

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The lever runs an order-up-to policy at three tiers, fed the real weekly demand of the 45 SKUs from session 1, summed. Each tier averages the last few weeks of what it sees, sets a target level for its lead time plus a 1.65 x spread buffer, and orders the change. Every number is computed in your browser. Northwick's chain is modelled with these rules; its real policy is constructed.

Read the base case. The lever opens on "Base case": an 8-week view, lead time 2 weeks, weekly orders. The three meters read 4.0x, 18.2x, 87.6x. In the chart, customer demand (solid) is the calmest line on the screen.

★ Base case stores 4.0x DC 18.2x supplier 87.6x variance of orders / variance of customer demand

Make every tier jumpier. Click "React to 4 weeks". Each tier now resets its target from only four weeks of history, so it chases every wobble: 6.6x, 50.4x, 411.7x. This is demand signal processing, and it is what a forecasting model that over-reacts to recent weeks does to the chain.

Slow the trucks. Click "Lead time 4 weeks": 6.0x, 40.1x, 289.6x. Nothing about the forecast changed. The longer the lead time, the bigger the target level, and the further each forecast change moves it.

Batch the orders. Click "Stores order monthly": the stores now order every 4 weeks. The stores' own ratio jumps to 9.2x, and the DC to 23.5x. The supplier, at 90.0x, was already swinging. Batching hurts most at the tier that batches.

Share sell-through. Click "Share sell-through data": every tier now forecasts from what customers bought. 4.0x, 9.2x, 16.5x. Then "Share data + 13-week view": 2.8x, 5.6x, 9.3x. Same demand, same lead time, a supplier swing cut by about nine tenths.

★ The repair share sell-through: 4.0x 9.2x 16.5x share + 13-week view: 2.8x 5.6x 9.3x (base was 4.0x 18.2x 87.6x)

Try one real SKU. Pick a single product in the series menu, for example a CZ item. One lumpy SKU swings far more than 45 summed, which is why disruption rules belong at the level a planner actually orders. Then go back to "All 45 SKUs together".

Write the disruption playbook. Copy this into section 4 of your own version of Northwick's playbook.

★ Playbook section 4 · the disruption playbook We share weekly sell-through and stock with DC North, DC South and our top suppliers, on one agreed definition. Every tier forecasts from customer demand, never from orders alone. Stores order weekly; no month-end batching to fill a truck. In a shortage we allocate on sell-through (session 3), so nobody gains by over-ordering. Lead time is a lever: we negotiate it before we buy sensing tools. Any sensing tool states which delay it cuts, in days, on our past disruptions, misses included. Owner: the supply chain lead. Constructed Northwick example on real UCI demand. Yours will differ.

Why the supplier's ratio is so large. The model forbids negative orders, so when a tier's target falls it simply stops ordering, then catches up in a lump. Real chains have the same floor. The absolute size depends on the policy you choose; the ranking of the levers is what transfers.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Measure your own whip. For one product family, compare the weekly variance of what customers bought with the variance of what you ordered from your supplier over the same weeks. The ratio is your bullwhip at one tier.
  2. Find the signal your tools learn from. For each forecast your team or a vendor runs, write down whether it is trained on customer demand or on orders received.
  3. List what you share. Which suppliers see your sell-through, how often, and on whose definition? Note one supplier where sharing would matter most.
  4. Draft section 4. Write your own disruption playbook in the lines of tonight's section, with a named owner.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the lever's base case, the supplier's orders vary 87.6 times as much as customer demand. Who made a mistake?

Every tier forecasts what it sees, adds a buffer and orders the difference. Each step is standard practice. The amplification comes from stacking them on a signal that is already a reaction.

2 · Sharing customer sell-through with every tier cuts the supplier's ratio from 87.6x to 16.5x. Why does the stores' ratio stay at 4.0x?

The first tier is fed by customers directly. Sharing changes what the DC and the supplier forecast from; their ratios fall from 18.2x to 9.2x and from 87.6x to 16.5x.

3 · A vendor demos a control tower that reads news and port data. Which question tests its claim best?

Sensing tools work on detection and decision delays. The claim is testable only as days saved on your own events, with the misses counted. No public evaluation of the category could be read, so your back-test is the evidence.
