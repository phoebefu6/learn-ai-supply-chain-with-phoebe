Part 0

The number a person is allowed to change

Four sections of Northwick's playbook are written: the error budget, the stock policy, the allocation rules and the disruption playbook. Every one of them assumes a forecast. Tonight's question is who may change that forecast, by how much, and how anyone will know whether the change helped. Northwick is a constructed mid-size homeware retailer: about 4,000 SKUs, DC North and DC South, about 60 stores and an online shop.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A way to score every step in the forecast chain against the one before it, what a study of more than 60,000 forecasts reports about which adjustments help, both sides of the argument about whether planners trust models too little or too much, and section 5 of Northwick's playbook: override rules with a reason code, a size, a log and an owner.

LiveWhy overrides get their own section3 min▶

In session 1 you dragged the override slider to +15 percent and watched two things happen at once: the learned model's bias shrank from -17.3% to -4.9%, and its WAPE rose from 77.5% to 79.8%, behind the free 8-week average. The planner fixed the lean and paid for it in accuracy on every SKU that did not need the help.

That is the whole tension of tonight in one slider. A planner knows things a model cannot see: a store refit, a supplier letter, a competitor closing down. A planner also has habits, moods and a boss who hates empty shelves. The override rules exist to keep the first and measure away the second.

Real world

The forecasting reference "Forecasting: theory and practice" (Petropoulos et al., International Journal of Forecasting, 2022) calls a step in the forecasting process that does not improve the forecast "process waste". Most planning teams have never measured whether their review meeting is one.

Part 1 · covers forecast value added

Score every hand on the forecast

6 min live

A forecast is rarely one number from one model. It passes through a chain: a statistical method, a planner's override, a consensus meeting. Forecast value added (FVA) scores each link. Petropoulos et al. define it as "the change in a forecasting performance metric that can be attributed to a particular step or participant", attributing the idea to Gilliland in 2002. You start from a naive forecast and ask, step by step, whether each one made the number better.

LiveScore every step, not just the model3 min▶

Most accuracy reports score one number: the final forecast against what sold. That tells you how good the whole chain is and nothing about which link earns its keep. FVA asks the same question at every handover:

  • Naive to statistical. Does the method beat "same as last week"? On the playground the 8-week average does, from 90.9% to 79.6%.
  • Statistical to learned. Does the AI step beat the best free method? It does, narrowly: 79.6% to 77.5%.
  • Model to planner. Does the override beat the forecast it overrode? A blanket +15% does not: 77.5% to 79.8%.
  • Planner to meeting. Does the consensus meeting beat what the planner sent in? Nobody at Northwick has measured it. That is the point of logging.

Petropoulos et al. call the steps that do not improve the forecast "process waste". Gilliland's own book on the method, "The Business Forecasting Deal" (Wiley, 2010), is reported to make the same case at length; we read the definition through the Petropoulos text, not the book.

Keep the sign too. FVA is usually run on an accuracy metric. Run it on bias as well: the +15% override has negative FVA on WAPE and positive FVA on bias. Both are true, and the rules in the build-along need both.

Self-studyNaive is the floor, not the bar3 min▶

FVA starts from naive because naive costs nothing: any step that cannot beat it is destroying value outright. But clearing the floor is not the same as earning a place. In session 1 the bar was set at the best spreadsheet method, the 8-week average at 79.6%, because that is what Northwick would run without buying anything. A vendor who reports its gain against naive is choosing the easier opponent.

So read an FVA table twice. Against naive, it tells you whether a step is waste. Against the step before, it tells you whether that step is worth its cost. How naive and seasonal-naive baselines are built is taught in Timeseries Forecasting: decomposition and baselines ↗.

Part 2 · covers when adjustments help

Big, rare and downward: what the evidence reports

5 min live

The largest field study of planners changing forecasts is Fildes, Goodwin, Lawrence and Nikolopoulos (International Journal of Forecasting, 2009). We have read its abstract, not its body, so everything below is what the abstract reports. Its shape is clear enough to write rules from: size and direction both matter, and they pull in different ways.

LiveWhat 60,000 forecasts are reported to show3 min▶

The abstract reports more than 60,000 forecasts and outcomes from four supply-chain companies. In three of the four, planners' adjustments improved accuracy on average. So the headline is not "planners make it worse". It is narrower and more useful:

  • Size. Larger adjustments tended to improve accuracy; smaller ones often reduced it. A big change usually means the planner knows something specific. A small one is often a nudge for comfort.
  • Direction. Upward adjustments were much less likely to improve accuracy than downward ones. The authors read this as optimism bias: people expect the product, the promotion or the season to do better than it will.

Exact percentages per company or per cell are not in what we read, so none are quoted. The rule-writing consequence does not need them: discourage small changes, and put a second pair of eyes on upward ones.

Real world

The same study is the reason many planning teams ask for a reason code with every override. If you cannot say why you changed the number, the abstract's findings suggest your change is more likely to be one of the small, hopeful ones that cost accuracy.

Self-studyWhy the playground rewards cutting4 min▶

Try both directions on the learned model in the build-along below. A -15% override gives WAPE 76.9%, better than untouched, and bias -29.7%. A +15% override gives WAPE 79.8% and bias -4.9%. Judged on WAPE alone, cutting wins.

That is not Fildes's result showing up in miniature. It is a property of the metric on lumpy demand. When a SKU sells nothing most weeks and a lot in a few, a forecast that sits low is wrong by a little on the many quiet weeks and by a lot on the few big ones, and that trade can lower the total absolute error while starving the shelves. WAPE throws the sign away; bias keeps it.

So hold two things side by side and do not force them together. The field study reports that downward adjustments were more likely to help, across 60,000 real forecasts. The playground shows that a downward adjustment can look like help on WAPE while deepening a shortfall that session 2 would turn into missed service. An override rule that watches only WAPE would reward exactly the wrong habit here. That is why section 5 scores bias as well.

Part 3 · covers algorithm aversion, appreciation and automation bias

Too little trust or too much: the research disagrees

6 min live

Will Northwick's planners ignore the model or follow it blindly? Two bodies of research give different answers, and the disagreement is not settled. One says people abandon an algorithm the moment they see it err. Another says lay people lean on algorithmic advice more than human advice. A third says that once people rely on automation, they miss its mistakes. The override rule has to work whichever way your team leans.

LiveFour findings that do not agree4 min▶
StudyWhat it foundWhat it predicts at Northwick
Dietvorst, Simmons, Massey 2015, five studiesAfter seeing an algorithm err, people were less likely to choose it over an inferior human forecaster, "even among those who saw the algorithm outperform the human". They lost confidence in algorithms faster than in people.One bad week on a visible SKU, and planners start overriding everything
Logg, Minson, Moore 2019, reportedAcross six experiments, lay people are reported to have relied more on advice labelled algorithmic than human. The effect weakened for forecasting experts and when the alternative was their own estimate.Junior staff may lean on the model; experienced planners, judging against their own number, may not
Parasuraman and Manzey 2010Automation bias produces both omission and commission errors when decision aids are imperfect. It occurs in naive and expert participants and "cannot be prevented by training or instructions".Once the model is trusted, a wrong forecast on a new range sails through review
Dietvorst, Simmons, Massey 2018People were more likely to use an imperfect algorithm when they could modify its forecasts, "even when participants were severely restricted" in how much.A small, bounded override keeps planners using the model

Aversion and appreciation point in opposite directions, and the studies differ in who was asked, about what, and against which alternative. Nobody has shown which one describes a professional planning team working with a forecast every week. Do not pick a side on a slide. Design so that either failure would show up in the data.

Real world

The design hint sits in the title of Dietvorst et al. 2018: people will use imperfect algorithms if they can "even slightly" modify them. A capped override is not a concession to planners' feelings. It is the cheapest known way to keep them using the model, and the log it produces is how you catch automation bias too: a planner who never overrides anything, on any SKU, for months, is a pattern to look at.

Self-studyWhy training alone will not fix it2 min▶

The tempting fix for either failure is a workshop: teach planners when to trust the model. Parasuraman and Manzey's review says automation bias cannot be prevented by training or instructions alone. Dietvorst's 2015 participants lost faith in an algorithm they had just watched beat a person. Neither failure is a knowledge gap.

What changes behaviour in both bodies of work is the structure of the task: what people are allowed to change, and what they see afterwards. That is why section 5 is written as rules and a monthly score, not as guidance.

Build-along

Northwick's override rules, scored on 45 real SKUs

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The same playground as session 1: five forecasters, 45 real products, 26 holdout weeks no method saw. This time the control that matters is the slider, "Planner override on every forecast", from -30% to +30%. You score each step the FVA way, try three overrides, and write section 5 of the playbook from what the numbers show.

Score the chain, step by step. With the slider at 0, pick "Naive (last week)": WAPE 90.9%. Then "8-week average": 79.6%. Then "Learned model": 77.5%. Each step beat the one before it, so each has positive FVA. Write the three numbers down; they are the untouched forecasts every override is scored against.

★ The untouched chain naive 90.9% -> 8-week avg 79.6% -> learned 77.5% FVA: +11.3 pts, then +2.1 pts (WAPE, lower is better)

Add the planner's blanket +15%. Keep "Learned model" and drag the slider to +15%. WAPE rises to 79.8%; bias moves from -17.3% to -4.9%. The override corrected a real lean and still has negative FVA on accuracy, because it pushed up every SKU, including the ones that were not short.

★ Override on a biased forecast learned, 0%: WAPE 77.5% bias -17.3% learned, +15%: WAPE 79.8% bias -4.9% FVA on WAPE: -2.3 pts. FVA on bias: closer to zero.

Same override, unbiased forecast. Pick "Exponential smoothing" at 0: WAPE 80.2%, bias -0.8%, almost no lean. Now drag to +15%: WAPE 85.3%, bias +14.1%. There was no lean to correct, so the override only added error. The same habit is half-useful on one model and pure waste on another.

★ Override on an unbiased forecast smoothing, 0%: WAPE 80.2% bias -0.8% smoothing, +15%: WAPE 85.3% bias +14.1% nothing to correct, so FVA is negative on both

Try the cut. Back to "Learned model", drag to -15%. WAPE "improves" to 76.9%, the best number you have seen tonight. Bias falls to -29.7%: forecasts now sit almost a third below what sold. A rule scored on WAPE alone would approve this override. Session 2's stock policy would turn it into empty shelves. The rule must watch both.

★ Why the rule watches two numbers learned, -15%: WAPE 76.9% (better than 77.5%) bias -29.7% (far worse than -17.3%) WAPE alone rewards forecasting low on lumpy demand.

Write the override rules into the playbook. Copy this into section 5 of your own version of Northwick's playbook. The rules come from the three studies and the four steps above; the thresholds are yours to set.

★ Playbook section 5 · override rules 1. Every override carries a reason code and a size, or it is not entered. 2. Small adjustments are discouraged: if it is not worth a reason, leave it. 3. Every override is logged and scored for FVA monthly against the untouched forecast, on WAPE and on bias, never WAPE alone. 4. Upward overrides need a second sign-off. 5. A bias the model already shows is corrected at source, in the model's level, never by a blanket override on every forecast. 6. A reason code that loses value two months running is retired. Owner: the demand planning lead. Constructed Northwick example on real UCI demand. Your numbers will differ.

Reason. The planner picks a code: a store refit, a supplier letter, a local event. No code, no override.

Size. The change is entered as a percentage of the model's number. Small ones are discouraged; upward ones go to a second person.

Logged. The untouched forecast is kept next to the overridden one. Without it, there is nothing to score against.

Scored. Next month, both are compared with what sold, on WAPE and on bias: the override's FVA.

Kept or cut. Reason codes that keep adding value stay. Ones that keep losing it are retired, by the demand planning lead.

Why the playground cannot settle tonight's argument. The slider applies one override to every SKU every week, which no planner does. Real overrides are targeted, and the field study's abstract reports that adjustments improved accuracy on average in three of four companies. What the playground does show cleanly is the mechanism: an override on a biased forecast trades bias for spread, an override on an unbiased one only adds spread, and WAPE alone can reward the wrong direction. Constructed Northwick rules on real UCI demand; your numbers will differ.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Draw your forecast chain. List every step between the first statistical number and the number that drives orders: models, planners, meetings, sign-offs. Mark which steps keep a copy of the number they received.
  2. Score one step. For one month and one category, compare the forecast before and after the planners' review with what sold. Compute WAPE and bias for both. Is the review's FVA positive on both, one, or neither?
  3. Split by direction. From the same month, separate upward from downward overrides and large from small. Does your team show the pattern the Fildes et al. abstract reports, or a different one?
  4. Draft section 5. Write your own override rules: reason codes, a size rule, who signs off upward changes, how often FVA is scored, and the owner.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Forecast value added scores each step in the forecast chain. What does it compare each step against?

FVA is the change in a metric attributable to one step or participant. You start from naive and ask whether each later step improved on the one before. On the playground: 90.9% to 79.6% to 77.5%, then a +15% override back up to 79.8%.

2 · What does the abstract of Fildes et al. 2009 report about upward versus downward adjustments?

Across more than 60,000 forecasts from four supply-chain companies, the abstract reports that upward adjustments were much less likely to help than downward ones. That is why section 5 puts a second sign-off on upward overrides.

3 · On the playground, a -15% override on the learned model "improves" WAPE from 77.5% to 76.9%. Why is it still a bad rule?

WAPE throws the sign away, and on lumpy demand forecasting low can lower it. Bias keeps the sign: -29.7% means a deeper shortfall than the untouched -17.3%. A rule scored on WAPE alone would approve the override that empties shelves.
