Part 1 · covers safety stock from error and lead time

A buffer is the error, priced

6 min live

You order the forecast plus a buffer. The buffer exists because the forecast misses, so its size comes from how far it misses (sigma), how much of the miss you choose to cover (z) and how long you wait for the next delivery (lead time, L). Three dials, one line: safety stock = z x sigma x sqrt(L).

LiveThe formula, one term at a time4 min▶
TermWhat it isWho sets it
zHow many sigmas of error you cover. At a 95 percent cycle service level z is 1.645; at 98 percent it is 2.054. Three more points of service cost a quarter more buffer.The business, as a price decision (Part 3)
sigmaThe forecaster's own one-week error spread, measured on weeks before the holdout. A better forecaster has a smaller sigma, which is the only way forecast accuracy turns into less stock.The forecast, measured, never assumed
sqrt(L)Lead time in weeks, square-rooted. You are exposed to every week of error until the next delivery lands.The supplier and the transport plan

Why the square root. If each week's error is independent of the last, the errors over L weeks add up in variance, not in sigma. Variance over L weeks is L x sigma squared, so the spread is sigma x sqrt(L). Double the lead time and the buffer grows by about 1.41x, not 2x. Errors that run in streaks break this assumption and need more stock than the formula says.

How sigma itself is measured, and why on a holdout, belongs to Timeseries Forecasting: measuring a forecast ↗. Here it is used, not retaught.

Real world

On the 45 real UCI SKUs, a flat 95 percent target with sigma from the 8-week average and a 2-week lead time gives safety stock worth GBP 11,854. Checked one week ahead on the 1,170 SKU-weeks of the holdout, it covered 93.3 percent: A items 96.4 percent, C items 89.0 percent. Same target, very different delivery by class. You will reproduce this in the build-along.

Self-studyBias is not in the formula3 min▶

The formula adds a symmetric buffer to the forecast. It assumes the forecast is right on average. Session 1's learned model is not: it runs 17.3 percent short. A buffer sized on spread is placed on top of a forecast that already sits low, so the lean eats part of the buffer every single week before any random miss arrives.

That is why the build-along's learned-model rung buys cheaper stock (its sigma is smaller) and delivers less service. Sizing cannot fix a lean. The order is: correct the level first, then size the buffer on what is left. And check the result the way session 1 checked accuracy, on weeks the policy never saw, because the holdout is the only place where the lean and the spread show up together.

A second reason the learned model looks cheap. On the bench its sigma is measured on weeks 52 to 77, which it was also trained on. A model's error on its own training weeks flatters it. Measure sigma on weeks the model did not learn from.

Part 2 · covers cycle service level vs fill rate

Two service numbers, one shelf

5 min live

"98 percent service" is two different promises depending on what is counted. Cycle service level counts order cycles that had no stockout at all. Fill rate counts units served out of units wanted. One short cycle can cost ten points on the first and less than one on the second.

LiveThe two definitions, side by side3 min▶
Cycle service levelFill rate
CountsOrder cycles with no stockout, out of all cyclesUnits served from stock, out of units wanted
IgnoresHow big the stockout was: 1 unit short and 100 short score the sameHow often it happened: many small misses can still read 99 percent
AnswersHow often does a customer meet an empty shelf?What share of demand did we serve?
Drives zDirectly: z = the normal quantile of the targetIndirectly, through expected units short per cycle

The stock widget in the build-along counts weeks, so its "achieved" is a cycle service level: of 1,170 SKU-weeks, how many were fully covered. It does not say how many units were missing in the weeks that were not.

The one-line test. When anyone quotes a service level, ask: cycles or units? Then ask: measured on which weeks?

Self-studyWhy vendors and planners quote different ones3 min▶

Both measures are standard. Silver, Pyke and Thomas (Inventory and Production Management in Supply Chains, 4th edition, CRC Press, 2017) teach them side by side, along with the expected units short per cycle, sigma_L x G(k), that connects them. The formulas here are the standard textbook forms, used rather than quoted.

Because fill rate is always at least as high as cycle service level for the same stock, a sales deck tends to quote fill rate and a store manager tends to feel cycle service level: she sees empty shelves, not missing percentages. Neither is wrong. A planning team that does not say which one it targets will argue for months about a gap that is only a definition.

Real world

The bench's flat 95 percent policy delivered a cycle service level of 89.0 percent on the real C items: roughly one week in nine with some shortfall. For a cheap, slow C item a short week is often a few units, so its fill rate would read much higher. Which number goes in the playbook decides whether C items look broken or fine.

Part 3 · covers the newsvendor trade-off

How much service is worth buying

6 min live

Every unit you stock is a bet in one direction or the other. Stock one too few and you lose its margin. Stock one too many and you pay to hold it or mark it down. The service level worth buying is where those two costs balance, and it is a ratio you can compute: Cu / (Cu + Co).

LiveService level is a price, not a virtue3 min▶

The newsvendor widget opens at Cu GBP 6 and Co GBP 2, constructed costs for a Northwick lantern, and prints 75.0% with a z of 0.67. That is the level at which one more unit of stock is expected to save as much lost margin as it costs in leftovers. Above it, every extra point of service costs more than it earns.

Now drag Co up to GBP 6, the same as Cu: the ratio falls to 50 percent and z to zero, stock equal to the mean forecast. Drag Cu to GBP 18 with Co at GBP 2: 90 percent. The number moves with the economics of the product, which is the point. A blanket "95 percent on everything" is a choice to pay the same price for service on a lantern with a fat margin and a card that marks down to nothing.

Say it in the meeting. "What does one lost sale cost us on this item, and what does one leftover cost?" If nobody knows, the service target is a habit, not a decision.

Self-studyA worked example, and tiering by ABC3 min▶

MIT OpenCourseWare 15.772J's lecture notes on the newsvendor inventory problem work an example against a critical fractile of 0.375. Read it at the source: a ratio below one half means the leftover costs more than the shortfall, and the right order sits below the mean forecast. The widget prints exactly that wording when you drag Co above Cu. Service below 50 percent can be the correct answer, and few planning teams ever write it down.

Tiering by ABC is a newsvendor decision made in bulk. A items carry most of the revenue, so a stockout there loses the most margin: a higher Cu, a higher ratio, a higher target. C items are cheap and often slow, so a leftover is relatively expensive: a lower target. The "98 / 95 / 90" tiers in the build-along are that logic in round numbers. They should be re-derived from your own margins, not copied.

The newsvendor is a one-period model: the leftover is lost or marked down. For staples that roll into next week, Co is mostly the holding cost, which pushes the ratio up. Session 6 prices holding cost at Northwick's constructed 25 percent a year.

Build-along

Northwick's stock policy, checked on 26 weeks it never saw

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The stock widget takes each forecaster's own one-week error from session 1, sizes safety stock for the same 45 real SKUs with z x sigma x sqrt(L), values it at the real average prices, and then checks it one week ahead on the 26 holdout weeks: 1,170 SKU-weeks, each covered or not. Everything is computed in your browser. Lead time is 2 weeks unless a step says otherwise.

Start flat. Click "95% on everything". Safety stock reads GBP 11,854 and service achieved 93.3%. Read the class meters: A items reach 96.4 percent against 95, but C items reach only 89.0 percent. One target, and the jumpy, low-value items fall short of it.

★ Flat 95%, 8-week average stock GBP 11,854 · achieved 93.3% A 96.4% (target 95%) · C 89.0% (target 95%)

Tier by ABC. Click "Tier by ABC: 98 / 95 / 90". Stock rises to GBP 14,184, GBP 2,330 more than flat, almost all of it on A items. A reaches 97.4 percent against a 98 percent target; C reaches 87.2 against 90. More money went where the margin is, and the policy still delivered less than it promised on every class it lifted.

★ Tiered 98 / 95 / 90 stock GBP 14,184 (+GBP 2,330 vs flat) · achieved 93.1% A 97.4% vs 98% · C 87.2% vs 90%

Swap in the learned model. Click "Same tiers, learned model". Stock falls to GBP 13,668: its sigma is smaller, so the same z buys a thinner buffer. But service falls too, to 92.1 percent, A 95.9, C 86.2. The learned model runs 17.3 percent short (session 1), and the buffer is spent covering the lean. Its sigma was also measured on weeks it trained on, which flatters it.

★ Same tiers, learned model stock GBP 13,668 · achieved 92.1% A 95.9% · C 86.2%: cheaper stock, worse service

Try the naive forecast. Click "Same tiers, naive forecast". The noisiest forecaster needs the most buffer: GBP 17,852, GBP 3,668 more than the 8-week average with the same tiers, for 93.8 percent achieved against 93.1. A 97.7, C 87.9. A worse forecast is paid for in stock, and the money buys very little service.

★ Same tiers, naive stock GBP 17,852 · achieved 93.8% A 97.7% · C 87.9%: most stock, little gain

Stretch the lead time. Click "Lead time 6 weeks". Stock jumps to GBP 24,567, which is the tiered GBP 14,184 x sqrt(6 / 2) = x sqrt(3), about 1.73x. Service achieved stays at 93.1 percent, exactly the tiered rung. That is not a free lunch: the widget's check is one week ahead and does not change with L, so it cannot show the extra exposure. Read this rung for the cost, not the service.

★ Playbook section 2 · the stock policy Service target per ABC class: A 98%, B 95%, C 90%, from our own Cu and Co. Target = cycle service level; fill rate reported beside it. Sigma = our own forecaster's error on holdout weeks, never a vendor's. Bias corrected before sizing: the learned model runs 17% short. Re-measure monthly: achieved vs target, per class. Owner: the inventory planning lead. Constructed Northwick example on real UCI demand. Yours will differ.

98% service on your A items.

z set for 98 percent, sigma from the fitted history. One number, no holdout, no word on which service.

97.4% delivered on 26 unseen weeks.

Cycle service level, one week ahead, 45 real SKUs. Short of the target the formula promised.

Why every target came in short. Sigma was measured on weeks 52 to 77; the check ran on weeks 78 to 103. The next stretch of demand is rarely as tame as the stretch you measured, so error measured on the past understates the error you meet next. The fix is not a bigger formula: it is to measure achieved service every month and raise sigma, or z, where a class keeps falling short.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Find your service definition. Look up the service target your team reports today. Is it cycle service level or fill rate, and over which weeks is it measured? If nobody can say, write that down.
  2. Price one mistake each way. For one A item and one C item, write down Cu (margin lost per unit short) and Co (cost per unit left over). Put both into the newsvendor widget and note the service level each one is worth.
  3. Check one promise. For one class, compare last quarter's service target with the share of weeks it was actually met. Is the gap the same sign as on the bench?

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Lead time goes from 2 weeks to 6. Error and service target stay the same. What happens to safety stock?

sqrt(6) / sqrt(2) = sqrt(3), about 1.73. On the bench the tiered policy goes from GBP 14,184 to GBP 24,567. Errors that run in streaks would need more than that.

2 · The tiered policy set z for 98 percent on A items and delivered 97.4 percent on the holdout. Why?

z = 2.054 is right for 98 percent. The sigma it multiplies came from weeks 52 to 77; the next 26 weeks were rougher. That is why the playbook re-measures achieved service every month.

3 · One unit too few loses GBP 6 of margin. One unit too many costs GBP 2. Which service level is worth buying?

The critical ratio is Cu / (Cu + Co) = 0.75, which the newsvendor widget prints as 75.0 percent with z 0.67. Above it, an extra point of service costs more in leftovers than it saves in lost margin.
